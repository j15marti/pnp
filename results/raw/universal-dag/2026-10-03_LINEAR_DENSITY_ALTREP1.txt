Interpretation of this declared elimination order:
Min-fill width is already linear in n at alpha=3, where the DPLL search was very easy.
Near alpha=4.25..4.5, the width coefficient rises only moderately, to about 0.67..0.69.
Therefore this elimination representation does not reproduce the DPLL easy-hard-easy peak.

Same-instance correlation:
Within each fixed (n,alpha) cell, correlations between min-fill width and log2(DPLL nodes+1)
are generally small and unstable in sign.
Pooled after z-scoring within every (n,alpha) cell:
Pearson correlation = 0.05881218976847351.
Cell-median correlation without controlling for n and density = 0.7702787955077423,
showing that the apparent global association is largely driven by common scaling with
problem size and density.

Important limitation:
Min-fill width is the width of one heuristic elimination ordering. It is an upper bound on
optimal induced width/treewidth, not a lower bound. The experiment therefore demonstrates
cost for this declared elimination representation, not hardness of all variable-elimination
orders and not hardness of arbitrary SAT algorithms.

Literature context:
- Dechter and Rish, Directional Resolution: The Davis-Putnam Procedure, Revisited (KR 1994):
  induced width and diversity control directional-resolution complexity.
- Dechter/Larrosa et al., Boosting Search with Variable Elimination:
  variable elimination has time/space exponential in induced width.
- Mitchell, Selman, Levesque (AAAI 1992), Crawford and Auton (AI 1996), and Freeman (AI 1996):
  random 3-SAT complete-search hardness peaks near the crossover.
- Gao and later random-graph treewidth results show linear treewidth in broad sparse-random
  graph regimes, qualitatively consistent with linear elimination width even away from the
  SAT crossover. This is analogy/context, not a direct theorem for the 3-SAT primal graph
  distribution used here.

Conclusion:
The earlier near-1.058^n DPLL finite fit does not survive as a common representation signal.
A different exact-reasoning representation exposes a broad linear-width bottleneck rather
than a narrow phase-transition peak. This weakens any attempt to interpret DPLL scaling as
representation-independent computational hardness.
