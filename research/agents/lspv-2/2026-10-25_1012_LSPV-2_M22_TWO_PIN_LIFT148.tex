\begin{lemma}[Pair-incidence accounting]\label{lem:inc}
For a two-regular four-triple support system \(H\) on six vertices and any distinct \(i,j\), let \(a_H\) be the number of its triples containing both. Then \(a_H\in\{0,1,2\}\), the number containing exactly one of \(i,j\) is \(4-2a_H\), the number avoiding both is \(a_H\), and the number containing \(i\) but not \(j\) is \(2-a_H\) (likewise with \(i,j\) exchanged).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}Each of \(i,j\) has incidence degree two. The total incidences among clauses with one or both names is four. Double-name clauses account twice, whence the exact-one count; subtract both categories from the four total clauses. The individual count follows from degree two.\end{proof}
\begin{theorem}[Two-pin robust six-variable extension, PROVED/EXACT restricted]\label{th:two}
For every \(F\in\CM\) on exactly six active variables, every distinct \(i,j\), and every \(b_i,b_j\in\{0,1\}\), there exists a satisfying assignment of \(F\) extending these two prescribed bits. When the bits differ, at least \emph{two} Hamming-weight-three extending assignments exist. When the bits agree, a balanced extending assignment exists except potentially in the explicit double-overlap pattern described in the proof. In that exceptional case there is still a nonbalanced extending assignment, constructible directly from the signed source supports.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A weight-three assignment corresponds to its true triple \(T\in{[6]\choose3}\). A positive clause with unsigned support \(S\in P\) is false on a balanced word precisely when \(T=[6]\setminus S\); a negative clause with support \(S\in Q\) is false precisely when \(T=S\).
First suppose \(x_i=1,x_j=0\). There are \(\binom42=6\) balanced true triples containing \(i\) and avoiding \(j\). Of these, positive failures correspond to the \(2-a_P\) positive supports that contain \(j\) but avoid \(i\); negative failures correspond to the \(2-a_Q\) negative supports that contain \(i\) but avoid \(j\). At most \(4-a_P-a_Q\le4\) of six candidates are excluded. Thus at least two survive. The reversed mixed assignment is symmetric.
Now suppose \(x_i=x_j=0\). There are \(\binom43=4\) balanced candidates. Positive failures arise only from positive clauses containing both \(i,j\), in number \(a_P\); negative failures arise only from negative clauses avoiding both, in number \(a_Q\), by Lemma~\ref{lem:inc}. If \(a_P+a_Q\le3\) at least one survives. If \(a_P=a_Q=2\), let the four other vertices be \(R\). Because positive degrees are exactly two, the two \(P\) clauses containing \(i,j\) have the forms \(\{i,j,a\},\{i,j,b\}\) for distinct \(a,b\in R\); the other two \(P\) clauses are forced to be \(\{a,c,d\},\{b,c,d\}\), where \(\{c,d\}=R\setminus\{a,b\}\). Similarly write the two \(Q\) pair-containing clauses using third vertices \(e,f\), with the two pair-avoiding clauses \(R\setminus\{e\}\) and \(R\setminus\{f\}\).
The four balanced words are indexed by their one omitted vertex in \(R\). The positive forbidden omissions are \(\{a,b\}\), while negative forbidden omissions are \(\{e,f\}\). If these sets intersect, at least one balanced word remains. Only if \(\{a,b\}\cap\{e,f\}=\varnothing\) can all four be forbidden. Then necessarily \(\{e,f\}=\{c,d\}\). Setting \(x_a=x_b=1\), \(x_c=x_d=0\), and \(x_i=x_j=0\) satisfies both \(P\) pair clauses, both \(P\) pair-avoiding clauses, all \(Q\) pair clauses, and both \(Q\) pair-avoiding clauses. This is a weight-two extension.
For \(x_i=x_j=1\), exchanging \(P,Q\) and complementing every assigned bit reduces the argument to the previous case. In the exceptional disjoint-pair pattern, the same four unpinned values \(x_a=x_b=1,\ x_c=x_d=0\), with \(x_i=x_j=1\), satisfy all eight clauses and have weight four. This completes every case. No algorithmic running-time lower bound was assumed.
\end{proof}
