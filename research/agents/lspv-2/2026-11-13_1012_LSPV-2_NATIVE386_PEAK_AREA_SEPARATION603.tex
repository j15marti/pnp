\paragraph{Theorem 603.1 (peak-one cut criterion).}
Call the gap after $t_{f,j}$ \emph{mixed} when at least one use of a distinct facet $g\ne f$ occurs strictly between $t_{f,j}$ and $t_{f,j+1}$. Write $r$ for the number of distinct facet values and $q$ for the total number of mixed gaps over all facet sequences. In the model above, provided all facet uses occur at distinct paid checkpoints, a nonoverlapping consecutive-use partition into $K$ nonempty views with peak at most one exists \emph{if and only if}
\[
 \boxed{K\ge r+q.}
\]
With exactly $K=r+q$, the unique peak-one cut set consists of the $q$ mixed gaps.

\paragraph{Proof.}
If a view spans two consecutive uses of $f$ separated by a use of $g\ne f$, the $f$ view remains live when $g$ is consumed; at least one $g$ view is simultaneously live. Thus peak one forces a cut at every mixed gap. Every one of the $r$ facet values requires an initial view; each cut creates one more, so $K\ge r+q$. Conversely cut every mixed gap and form just-in-time consecutive-use views. Were views of distinct values $f,g$ overlapping at a checkpoint, at least one of their consuming endpoints would lie within the other's interval. This would exhibit a mixed gap left uncut, contradiction. Views of one facet are successive, not overlapping. Hence peak one holds. With $K=r+q$, there is no view budget for any additional cut, proving uniqueness. For $K>r+q$, any additional splits remain peak-one. No claim is made about unconstrained changes of paid inference order or the full mixed rule calculus.
