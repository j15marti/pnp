\paragraph{Exact obstruction and established lower-bound anchor.}
Haken (1985) proves that for constants $c>0,h_0$, for all $h\ge h_0$, \emph{every} ordinary shared-DAG Resolution refutation of the explicit unary pigeonhole CNF $\mathrm{PHP}_{h+1}^{h}$ has at least $2^{ch}$ lines.
This CNF has $h(h+1)$ original variables, $(h+1)+h\binom{h+1}{2}$ clauses, and $\Theta(h^3\log(h+2))$ bits under an explicit binary-index signed-literal convention.
Subtracting the polynomial number of free source lines still gives exponentially many paid Resolution conclusions.
The theorem concerns ordinary Resolution, not Extended Resolution, Res($\oplus$), conservative DEF, mixed $R_{\mathrm{mix341}}$, or all uniform SAT algorithms.
Cook (1976) supplies polynomial Extended Resolution pigeonhole proofs; odd Tseitin has a compact GF(2) cancellation.
We use these as \emph{countercontrols}, not as hard computational examples.

\paragraph{Source and exact semantic problem.}
For $m\ge1$ and $b\in\{0,1\}^m$, define one fixed-format circuit encoding
$\mathrm{CIROR}\ m\backslash n\,b_1\cdots b_m\backslash n$.
The declared acyclic circuit is $o_1=b_1$, $o_j=o_{j-1}\lor b_j$, where each $b_j$ is independently stored as one constant bit; its output is constrained to equal $1$.
The fixed skeleton is implicit in the grammar, so total source length is $M=m+O(\log m)$, each bit at an independently addressable source position.
The circuit has a complete one-wire terminal interface, and its constrained output is SAT exactly when $\bigvee_j b_j=1$.
A compiler emits a \emph{self-contained explicit CNF} $E(b)$ (not a source pointer) and must preserve SAT status for all $b$, including $0^m$ and all single-bit flips. All invalid source strings are rejected.

\begin{theorem}[Cold-source acquisition, deterministic and randomized]\label{th:reads}
Every deterministic correct explicit-output compiler for this entire source grammar probes every one of the $m$ source-bit fields on input $0^m$, irrespective of output CNF size or cut width.
For randomized compilers which emit a SAT-equivalent CNF with error at most $\epsilon<1/2$ \emph{on every input}, the expected number of distinct source-bit probes on $0^m$ is at least $(1-2\epsilon)m$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Changing any single $0$ bit to $1$ changes the SAT label, while preserving source length, syntax, skeleton and interface width.
If a deterministic execution omits bit $i$ on the all-zero source, the run and emitted CNF are identical on the bit-flipped source, contradicting exact correctness.
For randomized execution couple the runs with the same random tape. On tapes not probing $i$ on zero, the emitted output is identical on both sources, so the two outputs cannot both have the correct SAT label.
The probabilities of error on the two inputs are each at most $\epsilon$; hence
$\Pr[\text{probe }i\mid 0^m]\ge1-2\epsilon$.
Sum this inequality over $i$ by linearity of expectation. No independence hypothesis is needed.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Fully charged interpretation.}
The inference proves $\Omega(m)=\Omega(M)$ \emph{source probes} in this explicit-copy compiler model, not hardness of circuit value on generic circuits and not a general SAT lower bound.
A deterministic correct producer scans $b$ until its first $1$ and emits $(x)$ (SAT), or after all zeros emits $(x)\wedge(\neg x)$ (UNSAT).
This uses $O(M)$ source probes, $O(\log M)$ writable bits for positions/headers, and constant-sized explicit output, attaining the read lower bound on all-zero input.
A lazy descriptor containing a pointer to the original source can be emitted cheaply but does not satisfy the theorem's independent explicit-CNF-output hypothesis: its downstream solver inherits the charged original-source reads.
A precomputed or recognized generator might bypass reading its own generated data; the theorem concerns arbitrary independently supplied source bits.
It therefore does not apply to the \emph{uniformly generated fixed decision circuit} of a hypothetical solver merely because that circuit happens to have $S=N^K$ gates.

\paragraph{Cold lazy-access exponent calculation.}
In a separately declared no-cache sequential-prefix adapter, querying child bit $j$ scans its first $j$ serialized bits.
Suppose a particular schedule has $b(N)=\lceil N^{\kappa}\rceil$ children, each of $L(N)=\lceil N^\lambda\rceil$ bits, and the downstream guests request every bit in increasing order.
Its exact source-probe count, excluding indexes and guests, is
\[
Q_{\rm cold}(N)=b(N)\sum_{j=1}^{L(N)}j
=\frac{b(N)L(N)(L(N)+1)}{2}
=\Theta(N^{\kappa+2\lambda}).
\]
At $\kappa=3/5,\lambda=1/2$ this is $\Theta(N^{8/5})$, which exceeds the $N^{11/10+o(1)}$ local-work target.
Direct indexed accesses cost at least $bL=\Theta(N^{11/10})$ accesses in this schedule, plus index-head and source-access overhead, while a one-time materialized child uses $\Theta(L)$ writable bits at its materialization peak.
If a lazy bit accessor requires a full $N$-bit parent scan per request, the specified schedule instead has $\Theta(bLN)=\Theta(N^{21/10})$ source probes.
These are \emph{implementation-specific costs}, NOT an unconditional lower bound: a guest might inspect fewer child bits, an indexed source might permit fast accessor reads, and shared preprocessing/caching may amortize repeated work. The exponents presuppose matching lower bounds on actual $b,L$, not only proposed upper budgets.
Every implementation must also charge grammar, selection, child assembly, repeated source/index reads, computation of predicates, guest runs, output and every local-phase peak $\rho$.
For materialized children the established bound remains $s'\le\max\{\rho,\eta,\lambda,\lambda s\}$.
Allocating $N$ writable bits even temporarily forces $\rho\ge1$.
