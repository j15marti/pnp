\begin{theorem}[Serial release versus indexed high-water]
Suppose $q$ successive $b$-bit children are materialized one at a time on a counted indexed work tape. If child $i$ is fully written/indexed into its own consecutive interval $[(i-1)b+1,ib]$, then clearing the previous child's interval can leave at most $b$ nonblank child bits at any instant, but the FLMV work-space accounting still charges at least $qb$ visited positions. If the same prefix $[1,b]$ is reused for every child, this particular buffer contribution is at most $b$, while both strategies perform $\Theta(qb)$ child-content writes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The largest ever indexed work-tape cell of the fresh-interval version is $qb$; erasing cells cannot undo an earlier indexed visit. In the reused-prefix version no child-buffer access visits beyond position $b$. Both can stream the same sequence of exact child contents, so semantics do not distinguish the allocation policies. The statement neither supplies those children nor emulates arbitrary guest machines.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}
With unconstructed ARC multiplicity $q=N^{3/5+o(1)}$ and physical child size $b=N^{1/2+o(1)}$, the fresh-interval policy incurs $\Omega(N^{11/10-o(1)})$ indexed work space, incompatible with $s<0.501132$. This condition is restricted to explicitly retained full buffers; compressed states require their own charged acquisition and accessor proofs.
\end{corollary}
