The single-tape approach yields a rigorous lower bound: for a conventional clause-list encoding, every deterministic single-tape 3-SAT decider requires
\boxed{T(N)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{N^2}{\log N}\right)}
on an infinite sequence of input lengths. This follows from a direct application of classical crossing-sequence arguments; I am not claiming it as a new literature result.

It also exposes a limitation: the bound already holds on an elementary, polynomial-time-solvable family.

1. A concrete family

For a,b\in\{0,1\}^k, construct an ordered clause list
F_{a,b}=A_a\land P_k\land B_b,
where
A_a=\bigwedge_{i=1}^k[x_i=a_i],
\qquad
B_b=\bigwedge_{i=1}^k[x_i=b_i].

Here P_k is a fixed satisfiable block on fresh variables, independent of a,b, occupying \Theta(k\log k) encoded symbols. For example, use k positive three-variable clauses on fresh variables.

With binary variable identifiers and fixed-width sign fields,
N_k=\Theta(k\log k),
and all inputs for a given k have the same length and block positions. Crucially,
F_{a,b}\in\mathrm{SAT}\iff a=b.

Unit clauses are allowed under the “width at most three” convention. If exactly three distinct variables per clause are required, replace each unit literal \ell by
(\ell\vee u\vee v)\land
(\ell\vee u\vee\neg v)\land
(\ell\vee\neg u\vee v)\land
(\ell\vee\neg u\vee\neg v),
using fresh auxiliary variables. This preserves the asymptotic size and the equality condition.

2. Why every boundary in the middle must carry information

Fix a correct deterministic one-tape machine M, with q\ge2 states.

For a boundary j inside P_k, let C_j(a) be its crossing sequence on the satisfiable input F_{a,a}.

Then
a\ne b\implies C_j(a)\ne C_j(b).

Otherwise, splice the left portion of the accepting computation for F_{a,a} with the right portion for F_{b,b}. Identical crossing sequences make the computations compatible, causing M to accept F_{a,b}, which is unsatisfiable.

This is precisely the classical crossing-sequence splicing principle, valid even when the machine overwrites its input. 

Consequently, each middle boundary must distinguish 2^k different accepting inputs.

3. Closing the “same input” gap

It is insufficient to find a different difficult input for each boundary. We can obtain one input difficult at every middle boundary.

Set
h=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2\log_2q}\right\rfloor.

There are fewer than q^{h+1} state sequences of length at most h. Injectivity therefore implies that, at any fixed boundary, at most
q^{h+1}\le q\,2^{k/2}
choices of a have a short crossing sequence.

There are only L=\Theta(k\log k) middle boundaries. By a union bound, the number of a’s having a short sequence at any middle boundary is at most
Lq\,2^{k/2}<2^k
for sufficiently large k.

Thus some a satisfies
|C_j(a)|>h=\Omega(k)
at every middle boundary simultaneously. Summing actual head movements gives
T_M(F_{a,a})
\ge\sum_{j\text{ in middle}}|C_j(a)|
=\Omega(k^2\log k)
=\Omega\!\left(\frac{N_k^2}{\log N_k}\right).

The hidden constant may depend on M. No workspace restriction was imposed.

4. What this establishes—and what it does not

Question	Result
Can a solver evade this bound by overwriting or rearranging the input?	No; those actions are included in its crossing sequences.
Can arbitrarily large tape space eliminate the bound?	No; the argument already allows it.
Does the argument require exponential search?	No; checking these formulas reduces to comparing a and b.
Does it force superlinear total space?	No; the restricted family can be decided using O(N) total tape space and polynomial time.
Does it contradict P=NP?	No; the lower bound remains polynomial.

5. The limitation for our expander route

At one input cut, a basic counting argument has only 2^{O(N)} possible input distinctions available. Distinguishing them requires at most O(N) bits of information. Summing that kind of information requirement over O(N) input boundaries produces, at best, a quadratic-scale argument.

This is a limitation of this counting method, not a theorem that all crossing-sequence methods stop at quadratic time.

An expander may force many dependencies across a cut, but that alone does not prove that the same information must be transported repeatedly. To go further, we need a justified reason why previously transferred information cannot be retained or replaced by a useful summary.

The concrete progress is an unrestricted near-quadratic one-tape lower bound, with the same-input issue resolved. The next missing ingredient is a repeated-work lower bound that survives arbitrary storage and preprocessing; expansion alone has not supplied it.
