\section{Results}
\begin{theorem}[Transfer-hardness ledger; PROVED/EXACT, elementary]\label{t:ledger}
If $\mathrm{LB}_R$ holds unconditionally, then $\mathrm{B3}_R\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{SAT}\notin\mathrm P$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The consequent of $\mathrm{B3}_R$ is false. An implication with a false consequent is
equivalent to the negation of its antecedent.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}[KNOWN-theorem instances]
For ordinary Resolution (Haken 1985; Urquhart 1987; Chvátal--Szemerédi 1988), Res($k$)
for constant $k$, Cutting Planes (Pudlák 1997; Hrubeš--Pudlák 2017), Polynomial Calculus
and Nullstellensatz, bounded-depth Frege (Ajtai; Beame--Impagliazzo--Krajíček--Pitassi--Pudlák--Woods),
regular and tree-like Res($\oplus$), and every ``multisign hypergraph'' reduction system whose
moves are Resolution/DP elimination steps (it is p-simulated by DAG Resolution),
the bridge B3 is \emph{literally equivalent to} P$\ne$NP. For these systems it is not a weaker
sufficient condition. The user's multisign DAG minimal-work measure falls in this class.
\end{corollary}
\begin{theorem}[Opposite monotonicity; PROVED/EXACT]\label{t:mono}
Suppose $R'$ p-simulates $R$, with cost polynomially related to encoded length on both sides.
Then $\mathrm{B3}_R\Rightarrow\mathrm{B3}_{R'}$ and $\mathrm{LB}_{R'}\Rightarrow\mathrm{LB}_R$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For both statements: $\overline W_{R'}(N)\le\operatorname{poly}(N+\overline W_R(N))$.
\end{proof}
So the bridge becomes weaker as $R$ gets stronger, while the lower bound gets harder.
The only systems where the program splits P$\ne$NP into two pieces, neither known false
nor known equivalent to P$\ne$NP, are those with \emph{no} known superpolynomial bound:
general Res($\oplus$) (open, unless the Braun claim is confirmed), AC$^0[p]$-Frege, TC$^0$-Frege,
Frege, EF, and $R_{\mathrm{mix341}}$. For EF and $R_{\mathrm{mix341}}$ the bridge is
CONDITIONAL on reflection, i.e. polynomial-time correctness provable in PV (Cook 1975;
corpus 357/361). For every system, the lower-bound half is a long-standing open problem.
