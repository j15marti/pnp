\section{The palette transfer and its exact obstruction}
Let $G_P$ be the positive-primal simple graph, connecting two original
variables when they occur in a common positive clause. Every vertex
belongs to two positive triples, so $\Delta(G_P)\le4$. A $K_5$ component
would contradict $3|P_C|=2|V_C|$ on its five vertices, since all degree
four would already be used. Constructive Brooks four-coloring, as
established in source-level Q2-FOURCOLOR140, therefore supplies in
polynomial time a proper $c:V\to\{0,1,2,3\}$. Every positive triple has
three distinct $c$-colors.

For any false color pair $Z\in {[4]\choose2}$, define the Boolean
source assignment $x_v=0$ if $c(v)\in Z$, and $x_v=1$ otherwise.
Every positive triple has at least one TRUE and one FALSE name, and
every negative clause whose unsigned support belongs to $H$ is also
satisfied. For each $B\in Q'$, put
$A_B=\{c(v):v\in B\}\subseteq[4]$.

\begin{lemma}[Three-set pair hitting; exact finite combinatorics]
Let $A_1,A_2,A_3$ be nonempty subsets of a four-element universe.
No two-element set intersects all three if and only if, after
permuting their indices, $A_1=\{a\}$ and $A_2=\{b\}$ with
$a\ne b$, while $A_3\cap\{a,b\}=\varnothing$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The displayed configuration forces the unique candidate
$\{a,b\}$ for the first two singleton constraints and it misses the third.
Conversely, if no $A_i$ is singleton, choose a two-element subset
$e_i\subseteq A_i$ for each $i$. Those three edges form a graph with
at most three edges on four vertices. Such a graph has two
nonadjacent vertices, since only $K_4$ has every pair adjacent.
Their complementary pair intersects all $e_i$, hence all $A_i$.
If there is exactly one distinct singleton color $a$, and two
other nonsingleton sets both omit $a$, those sets have size at least
two in a three-element universe and intersect; use $a$ and a
common element. Otherwise $a$ together with a suitable element
hits the remaining sets. Several singletons of one color are
handled identically. Finally, at least two distinct singleton
colors force their pair, and failure can only come from the
remaining set avoiding both.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Source-consistent q=3 palette and grid theorem]
Fix \emph{any} valid $F\in\CM$ with $q(F)=3$ and any proper
four-coloring $c$ of $G_P$.
If no pair of color names $Z$ satisfies all negative $Q'$ clauses,
then the three $P'$ supports are mutually disjoint, the three $Q'$
supports are mutually disjoint, and, after indexing these triples
$P'_1,P'_2,P'_3$ and $Q'_1,Q'_2,Q'_3$,
\[
     |P'_i\cap Q'_j|=1\qquad(1\le i,j\le3).       \tag{2}
\]
Consequently every valid $q=3$ input for which either $P'$ or
$Q'$ contains two intersecting triples is satisfiable and admits
a polynomial-time source-readable satisfying assignment.
In particular, every valid six-variable $q=3$ source admits
such a satisfying assignment. An UNSAT $q=3$ source, if one exists,
must have the nine-variable grid structure of (2).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first two groups of clauses are satisfied for every $Z$ as
observed. Negative unmatched clause $B$ is satisfied precisely
when $Z\cap A_B\ne\varnothing$. By the lemma, failure of all six
pairs implies two negative unmatched triples $B_1,B_2$ monochromatic
in distinct colors $a,b$, and a third $B_3$ containing neither
$a$ nor $b$.

All three distinct $a$-colored names in $B_1$ occur at least once
in $P'$, by (1). Each $P'_i$ contains at most one $a$-colored name
because $c$ is a proper positive-primal coloring, and there are
exactly three $P'$ triples. Hence each of those three names
occurs \emph{exactly once} in $P'$, one name in each $P'_i$.
The same argument applies to the three distinct $b$-colored names
in $B_2$, giving one in each $P'_i$.
One position remains in each $P'_i$, all of a color outside
$\{a,b\}$. The three distinct names in $B_3$ must each occur
at least once in $P'$ by (1), so they fill these three remaining
positions exactly once each. Thus all nine defect names are
distinct, every $P'_i$ meets every $B_j=Q'_j$ once, and each
unmatched family consists of three pairwise disjoint triples.
If these combinatorial necessary conditions fail, some $Z$ works.
Source validation, computation of $q,P',Q'$, constructive Brooks
coloring, six candidate tests and a final original-source witness
verification have polynomial bit-work and require no label oracle.
The last conclusion follows because three disjoint ternary
supports require at least nine different active variables.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Important boundary of the theorem.}
The grid is necessary for failure of a \emph{chosen} coloring and
necessary for possible UNSAT at $q=3$. It is not sufficient for
UNSAT, nor does it show that every alternative coloring fails.
It supplies no $q=3$ UNSAT instance and no lower bound on
arbitrary adaptive SAT decision.
