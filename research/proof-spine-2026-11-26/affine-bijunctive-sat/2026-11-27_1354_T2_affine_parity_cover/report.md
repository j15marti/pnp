## Scoped theorem: affine compression of guard complement
Let A be a verified affine carrier over GF(2), containing all solutions of current exact context. Let checked conditional contradictions exclude G1..Gk, where k fixed and each Gi affine in same coordinates. Fix an equation from each Gi violated by an uncovered assignment. Partition A minus Gi into disjoint affine cells by first failed equation; composing for k guards gives at most max(1,r)^k cells, for at most r independent equations per guard, with inconsistent cells dropped by GE. On each nonempty cell take one point p and a linear basis of directions; the affine hull of their union is spanned by all p and p+d. All actual feasible words lie in this hull, so compute all affine equations valid on the entire uncovered set by ordinary GE. These equations can be added unconditionally and substituted with checked reverse maps. This is polynomial for fixed k including affine cell construction and hull calculation, NOT an exact replacement of uncovered set by its hull, NOT a universal guard acquisition theorem. Preserve complement constraints or original constraints; hull may contain excluded words. For the five-cell construction used here, checking 8 local assignments is enough; no free enumeration of r-dimensional chart. Rank-one equality removes one carrier, with exact pullback; a new global potential must account for charts, all proof obligations and reused credits.
Audit of T1 rank firewall: in irredundant affine cover by k guards, choose exclusive point a in guard Gi. For every j != i choose an affine defining equation of Gj false at a; intersect their complements to get an affine flat through a disjoint from all other guards, contained in Gi by coverage. Its codimension <=k-1, hence codim(Gi)<=k-1. This proves per-guard bound, not a polynomial number of candidate normals. The joint rank bound from this elementary argument is at most k(k-1), not k-1; stronger theorem needs independent exact hypotheses. Dense rank-one normal pool contains 2^r-1 possibilities. The failed sparse-normal sample is not a proof that none exist.
