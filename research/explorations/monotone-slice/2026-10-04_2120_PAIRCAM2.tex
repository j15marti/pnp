\section{Literature update}

The literature search did not locate a rigorous characterization of pair-orbit
correlations for random 3-SAT conditioned on UNSAT in the intermediate regime
between the pure-literal and satisfiability thresholds.

Scott--Sorkin explicitly leave this intermediate rare-UNSAT regime open.
Caragiannis--Gravin--Jiang show that bounded $k$-wise independence can behave
very differently from fully independent random 3-SAT; their fixed-$k$
satisfiability theorem does not reach linear clause density.  Ahmadi et al.
develop quiet planting for satisfiable $k$-SAT and obtain statistical-query
indistinguishability by controlling low-order clause statistics.  This is a
methodological analogy, not a monotone-circuit transfer.

Older empirical work of Lynce--Marques-Silva connects UNSAT-core/backdoor size
to search complexity in random 3-SAT, but does not resolve the conditioned
rare-event asymptotics needed here.

Thus pair camouflage remains a necessary hostile test rather than a theorem
of hardness.

\section{Exact slice-predicate guard}

Let $P_q$ be any predicate on the exact Hamming layer
$\binom{[M]}t$ computable by polynomial-size unrestricted circuits.  Define
the total slice extension
\[
H_q(z)=
\begin{cases}
0,&|z|<t,\\
P_q(z),&|z|=t,\\
1,&|z|>t.
\end{cases}
\]
Because distinct inputs of weight $t$ are incomparable, $H_q$ is monotone.
Valiant's slice theorem therefore gives a polynomial-size monotone circuit for
$H_q$.

Consequently, not only one-sided monotone pattern thresholds but \emph{any}
polynomial-size pair-window classifier on the exact layer is a legitimate
small-monotone hostile distinguisher.  Pair camouflage must therefore defeat
two-sided polynomially computable pair tests as well.

\section{Full pair-orbit experiment}

The clause-pair orbits under signed variable permutations are indexed by
\[
(s,h),
\]
where $s$ is the number of shared variables and $h$ is the number of shared
variables with opposite signs.  The nine distinct-clause orbits are
\[
(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(2,0),(2,1),(2,2),(3,1),(3,2),(3,3).
\]

At density
\[
t=3.5q,
\]
20,000 exact distinct-clause formulas were sampled independently for each
$q\in\{20,24,28,32\}$.  The stable dominant differences were:

\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
q&\Delta(1,0)&\Delta(1,1)&\Delta(2,0)&\Delta(2,1)\\
\hline
20&-10.12&+15.68&-2.08&+4.98\\
24&-13.16&+20.14&-2.52&+4.80\\
28&-14.04&+19.94&-2.10&+5.17\\
32&-16.20&+26.70&-2.41&+5.15
\end{array}
\]
where each entry is UNSAT mean minus SAT mean in the corresponding orbit
count.

Measured in SAT standard deviations, the $(1,1)$ excess is approximately
$0.8$--$1.0$ standard deviations and the $(1,0)$ deficit approximately
$0.5$--$0.6$ standard deviations across the tested sizes.  The $(2,1)$ excess
is weaker but also stable.  The $s=3$ orbits are sparse and showed no stable
separation.

This localizes the earlier scalar
\[
T(F)=\sum_i d_i^+(F)d_i^-(F)
\]
signal: rare UNSAT formulas preferentially increase opposite-sign overlap at
shared variables.

\section{Train/holdout pair camouflage}

A separate run used 40,000 formulas per $q$, split into 20,000 training and
20,000 holdout samples.  Pair-window parameters were estimated from
\emph{training SAT samples only}.  A formula was called pair-camouflaged when
the six dominant orbit counts
\[
(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(2,0),(2,1),(2,2)
\]
all lay within $1.5$ training-SAT standard deviations of their respective
training-SAT means.

Holdout survival was:

\[
\begin{array}{c|cc|cc}
q&\#SAT&\#UNSAT&\Pr[\mathrm{cam}|SAT]&\Pr[\mathrm{cam}|UNSAT]\\
\hline
20&19563&437&0.4720&0.3684\\
24&19679&321&0.4612&0.3676\\
28&19766&234&0.4744&0.3504\\
32&19835&165&0.4767&0.2970
\end{array}
\]

Thus pair camouflage retains a nontrivial finite UNSAT population, but the
window itself still has a visible SAT/UNSAT survival gap.  By the slice-
predicate guard, if such a gap stayed bounded away from zero asymptotically,
the raw conditioned-UNSAT distribution would already be defeated by a
polynomial-size monotone circuit.

No asymptotic persistence is claimed.

\section{Third-order probe after pair camouflage}

On the pair-camouflaged population define
\[
U_3(F)=
\sum_i\left[
\binom{d_i^+}{2}d_i^-+
d_i^+\binom{d_i^-}{2}
\right].
\]
This is a degree-three local-pattern count.

The threshold was calibrated from pair-camouflaged training SAT samples only:
\[
U_3\ge
\mathbb E_{\rm train,SAT}[U_3]
+
0.5\,\mathrm{SD}_{\rm train,SAT}(U_3).
\]

Held-out balanced accuracy on the pair-camouflaged population was:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
q&\mathrm{balanced\ accuracy}\\
\hline
20&0.5932\\
24&0.6034\\
28&0.5509\\
32&0.6496
\end{array}
\]

The cubic signal is therefore weaker and not monotone in $q$ over this range.
This is not evidence of an asymptotic order-three gap.  It does show that pair
conditioning does not automatically erase every higher-order finite signal.

\section{Multi-order deletion-minimal core check}

A further independent run used 20,000 formulas per $q$, with a training/holdout
split for the same pair window.  For every pair-camouflaged holdout UNSAT
formula, 20 independent random clause-deletion orders were run.  Each order
was reduced to a deletion-minimal UNSAT core, and the smallest core found over
the 20 orders was recorded.

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|ccc|ccc}
q&\#\mathrm{cam\ UNSAT}&
\overline m_{\rm best}&m_{\min}&m_{\max}&
\overline n_{\rm best}&n_{\min}&n_{\max}\\
\hline
20&78&30.97&22&40&17.74&14&20\\
24&62&38.77&30&49&21.35&17&24\\
28&41&46.17&28&56&24.54&18&27\\
32&23&56.22&46&64&29.04&25&32
\end{array}
\]

No tiny core was found in these pair-camouflaged samples.  The best cores
found still use roughly $0.88$--$0.91$ of the variables on average.

This is only a heuristic upper bound on the minimum MUF size: failure to find a
smaller core after 20 deletion orders does not prove one is absent.

\section{Interpretation}

The experiment gives a sharper finite hierarchy:

\[
\text{first order: exact camouflage by symmetry;}
\]
\[
\text{pair order: clear finite signal;}
\]
\[
\text{pair-conditioned order three: weaker, inconsistent finite signal;}
\]
\[
\text{pair-conditioned cores: still macroscopically large in the cores found.}
\]

The main negative result is that uniform conditioned UNSAT is not yet a clean
positive distribution: even a six-orbit pair window accepts SAT and UNSAT at
noticeably different finite rates.

The main positive result is that a substantial UNSAT subpopulation survives
pair camouflage, and the best deletion-minimal cores found in that
subpopulation remain large.

\section{Next experiment}

The next experiment should not merely add another scalar statistic.  It should
construct a \textbf{pair-matched positive distribution}.

Recommended procedure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Partition the dominant six-orbit vector into coarse cells whose widths
are calibrated only from SAT training data.
\item Reweight or rejection-sample UNSAT formulas so that the positive and
negative distributions have the same empirical cell distribution on held-out
data.
\item Test all constant-order three-clause signed-overlap statistics on a
fresh holdout set.
\item In parallel, run stronger minimum-core search on the matched positive
population at the smaller values of $q$.
\end{enumerate}

If a fixed-order statistic still distinguishes with constant advantage, it is
again a polynomial-size slice predicate and therefore a small-monotone hostile
circuit after Valiant.  If all order-three tests collapse while a large-core
positive population survives, that would be the first setting in this branch
worth feeding into SAT-specific sunflower machinery.
